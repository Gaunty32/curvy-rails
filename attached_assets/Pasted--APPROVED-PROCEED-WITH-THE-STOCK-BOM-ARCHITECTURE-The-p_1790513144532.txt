# APPROVED — PROCEED WITH THE STOCK / BOM ARCHITECTURE

The proposed architecture is approved.

You have correctly understood the fundamental model:

**SELLABLE PRODUCT ≠ NECESSARILY A STOCKED FINISHED PRODUCT**

For our core website range we will predominantly stock:

**BLANK GARMENTS + DECORATION COMPONENTS**

and manufacture the finished item after the customer orders.

Please proceed on the following basis.

## 1. EXISTING SYSTEM

Reuse and extend the existing:

* product/variant management
* supplier functionality
* purchase orders
* partial PO receipts
* picking
* worksheets
* despatch
* order allocation

Do not create a parallel operational system where the existing functionality can safely be extended.

Preserve existing historical data.

---

# 2. BOM MODEL — APPROVED

Proceed with a separate manufacturing BOM model.

A sellable variant can require:

* one or more physical components
* one or more production operations
* packaging where appropriate

Physical components include things such as:

* blank garments
* DTF transfers
* patches/badges
* packaging

Non-stock production requirements include things such as:

* embroidery design
* embroidery position
* heat press operation
* application position
* production instructions

Do NOT pretend an embroidery design is physical inventory.

---

# 3. SHARED COMPONENTS — ESSENTIAL

Shared component inventory is fundamental.

For example:

**Black Hoodie Size 22**

may be used to produce 20 different website designs.

There must be ONE underlying blank stock balance.

Do not create separate blank inventory for each sellable design.

Likewise, a transfer may be shared across multiple garment products where appropriate.

---

# 4. OUR STOCK PHILOSOPHY

Our objective is:

# HOLD BLANKS AND DECORATION — NOT FINISHED GARMENTS.

We want to maintain sufficient blank/component stock to allow the majority of website orders to move immediately into production.

The operational target is:

**ORDER TODAY → PRODUCE QUICKLY → DESPATCH QUICKLY**

Therefore stock management should eventually help us optimise component holding based on actual demand.

---

# 5. ORDER-TO-REPLENISH POLICY

Use two distinct concepts:

## A. READY TO PRODUCE

All required physical components are currently available.

The order can immediately proceed.

## B. AVAILABLE TO ORDER

One or more components are not currently available, BUT the product has explicitly been configured to allow ordering against replenishment.

This must be a deliberate admin setting.

Do NOT automatically allow every out-of-stock component to be sold.

Each component/product should eventually support appropriate lead-time information.

If a customer orders something requiring replenishment, the promised customer delivery expectation must reflect this.

---

# 6. WEBSITE AVAILABILITY

For products configured as READY TO PRODUCE only:

Website availability is constrained by the lowest available required physical component.

Example:

Black Hoodie Size 22 = 6 available

Good Vibes Transfer = 23 available

Immediate producible quantity:

**6**

However, because the blank is shared by multiple designs, do NOT imply that six units are independently available for every design.

All confirmed orders/holds must compete for the same underlying component inventory.

---

# 7. CHECKOUT RESERVATION

Proceed with component-level temporary reservations during checkout.

The reservation should have a configurable expiry.

Successful payment:

TEMPORARY HOLD

→

CONFIRMED ALLOCATION

Failed/abandoned payment:

TEMPORARY HOLD

→

RELEASED

Payment callbacks must be idempotent.

A duplicated payment callback must never:

* create another order
* allocate stock twice
* consume stock twice.

---

# 8. CONFIRMED ORDER

On confirmed payment:

1. Snapshot the BOM used at the point of sale.
2. Snapshot the production operations/instructions.
3. Allocate available physical components.
4. Calculate any shortfall.
5. Create the production job.
6. Determine whether the job is READY or WAITING FOR COMPONENTS.
7. Add shortages to purchasing requirements.

Historical orders must retain their original BOM even if the product BOM is subsequently changed.

This is important.

---

# 9. PURCHASING — IMPORTANT DECISION

Purchasing must distinguish between:

## CUSTOMER SHORTFALL

Stock required to fulfil an actual paid order.

This is highest priority.

and:

## REPLENISHMENT

Stock required to return inventory to our preferred holding level.

Example:

Confirmed orders require: 8

Available stock: 5

Incoming confirmed PO: 0

Customer shortfall: 3

Target stock after fulfilment: 12

Suggested purchase:

**15**

Broken down visibly as:

**3 — customer orders**

**12 — stock replenishment**

Staff must be able to amend this before creating/approving the PO.

---

# 10. EXISTING INCOMING STOCK

Confirmed incoming PO quantities must be considered.

Do not suggest purchasing the same requirement twice.

Example:

Shortfall = 10

Already ordered from supplier = 8

Additional requirement = 2

The dashboard should make this obvious.

---

# 11. GOODS IN

Extend the existing PO receipt workflow.

When components arrive:

RECEIVE GOODS

↓

Increase physical component stock

↓

Update incoming stock

↓

Reassess allocations/shortfalls

↓

Allocate newly available components to waiting confirmed orders

↓

Production jobs that now have everything required become:

# READY TO PRODUCE

Prioritise older/due confirmed orders when allocating newly received stock.

---

# 12. EXISTING FINISHED STOCK

Do NOT automatically reinterpret existing variant stock as component stock.

Preserve existing quantities/history.

During migration, classify inventory explicitly as appropriate:

* FINISHED STOCK
* COMPONENT STOCK
* LEGACY / REQUIRES REVIEW

We will reconcile opening component balances before the new component stock becomes authoritative.

Do not destroy or silently transform historical inventory.

---

# 13. PRODUCTION

Proceed with:

WAITING FOR COMPONENTS

↓

READY TO PRODUCE

↓

PICKING

↓

PICKED

↓

IN PRODUCTION

↓

QUALITY CHECK

↓

READY FOR DESPATCH

↓

DESPATCHED / COMPLETE

Keep exceptions/rework explicit.

---

# 14. PRODUCTION WORKSHEET

This needs to be exceptionally simple.

The person producing the order should not need to understand the website order or BOM.

The worksheet should tell them:

# WHAT TO MAKE

Order number

Customer/order reference

Product image

Garment

Colour

Size

Quantity

# WHAT TO APPLY

Decoration image/thumbnail

Decoration reference

Method

Position

Any production-specific instructions

Include a job barcode/QR-ready identifier.

Do NOT clutter the worksheet with:

* customer pricing
* supplier pricing
* marketing information
* irrelevant order data.

---

# 15. BATCHING

Build the data model to support production batching.

Initially this can be suggested rather than fully automated.

Examples:

### DTF

Group compatible work by:

* transfer/design
* application method
* position

### EMBROIDERY

Group compatible work by:

* embroidery design
* thread setup
* position
* suitable machine

However, each physical item must retain its:

order

order line

production job

customer traceability.

---

# 16. PRODUCTION FAILURE

If production damages a component:

Example:

Operator damages Black Hoodie Size 22 while applying transfer.

Record:

PRODUCTION DAMAGE

Physical blank stock reduces by 1.

The original order still requires one completed hoodie.

Therefore:

allocate another blank if available

OR

create/reveal a component shortfall.

Likewise, if the transfer has been destroyed, consume/damage that transfer appropriately and require another.

Never hide these failures by manually changing stock quantities.

---

# 17. QC

QC should be quick:

**PASS**

or

**PROBLEM**

PASS:

→ READY FOR DESPATCH

PROBLEM:

→ appropriate rework/exception route.

Record who performed QC and when.

---

# 18. DESPATCH

Only allow an order to move to READY FOR DESPATCH once all required order items have passed the necessary production/QC stages.

Generate the customer-facing delivery note at that point.

The delivery note contains the products the customer bought.

It must NOT expose:

* component SKUs
* BOM
* supplier information
* component costs
* production instructions.

---

# 19. STOCK MOVEMENT DISCIPLINE

Once the new component system becomes authoritative:

Do not allow arbitrary stock quantity editing without creating a movement.

Every change must have a reason.

Examples:

GOODS_RECEIPT

PRODUCTION_CONSUMPTION

PRODUCTION_DAMAGE

CUSTOMER_RETURN

STOCKTAKE_ADJUSTMENT

MANUAL_ADJUSTMENT

CANCELLATION

etc.

A manual adjustment requires:

quantity

reason

user

timestamp

optional note.

---

# 20. STOCKTAKE

Proceed with component-level stocktaking.

Expected quantity

vs

Counted quantity

=

Variance

Any approved variance creates an auditable stock movement.

---

# 21. COSTING

Use the BOM to calculate estimated cost of the sellable product.

Keep these cost elements separately identifiable:

Blank garment

Decoration

Packaging

Production/labour allowance

Other component costs

This allows us later to analyse:

SELLING PRICE

minus

TRUE PRODUCT COST

=

CONTRIBUTION / MARGIN

Do not assume that component purchase cost alone represents finished product cost.

---

# 22. FUTURE STOCK INTELLIGENCE

Do NOT build automatic purchasing intelligence yet.

But make sure the data model captures enough information so we can later calculate:

* units sold by component
* average daily/weekly usage
* supplier lead time
* days of cover
* stockout frequency
* demand by size
* demand by colour
* demand by garment
* demand by decoration
* production failure/waste rate

Eventually we want Curvy Rails to recommend:

**what to buy, how many, and when.**

That comes later.

# 24. REQUIRED TEST SCENARIOS

Before considering this complete, demonstrate automated/integration tests for at least:

1. Two different designs competing for the same blank garment.
2. One design available across multiple garment sizes.
3. Insufficient blank stock but sufficient transfer stock.
4. Sufficient blank stock but insufficient transfer stock.
5. Both components unavailable.
6. Existing incoming PO partially covering a shortage.
7. Partial PO receipt.
8. Remaining PO receipt.
9. Two customers attempting to buy the final available component simultaneously.
10. Abandoned checkout releasing its reservation.
11. Successful payment converting hold to allocation.
12. Duplicate payment callback not allocating twice.
13. Customer cancellation correctly releasing components.
14. Production damage creating a replacement requirement.
15. BOM changed after an earlier order without changing the historic order.
16. Stocktake variance creating the correct ledger movement.
17. Shared component availability updating every product dependent upon it.
18. Order becoming production-ready automatically after required goods are received.

Do not test this only through happy-path UI flows.

Test the inventory invariants at database/service level.
